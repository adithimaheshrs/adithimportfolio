Chapter 01

The problem.

— Money leaves quietly, every month.

— the problem
No single place to see it all.

Every month, money leaves your account quietly. Sometimes for Netflix. Sometimes for a service you signed up for six months ago and completely forgot about. You only find out when you check your bank statement, and by then it's already gone.

Keeping track of subscriptions is genuinely hard. They renew automatically, they charge at different times of the month, they're spread across multiple cards, and cancelling them usually means hunting down a buried settings page on each individual platform. There was no single place to see everything, understand what you're actually spending, and do something about it.

UnSub was my attempt to build that place.

Chapter 02

Understanding
the problem.

— Discover, Define, Ideate, Design, Test.

— research
A gap right there in the comparison.

I followed a full design thinking process, Discover, Define, Ideate, Design, Test, across two weeks. Market research confirmed the scale of the problem: the streaming subscription market in India alone has tens of millions of users across services like Disney+ Hotstar, Amazon Prime, Netflix, Spotify, and dozens of others.

60%
of users reported spending more on streaming services than on movie theatres.
45.7%
said they spend more time on streaming than on social media.

User interviews sharpened the picture. I asked four key questions: how many subscriptions do you have? How often do you track them? Are they all on the same payment method? Do you know your total monthly or yearly spend? The answers were consistent: most people had more subscriptions than they thought, tracked them rarely or not at all, and had no clear sense of what they were spending in total.

Competitive analysis across five existing apps (Bobby, Subscription Manager, Fleek, Dinero, and Recure) revealed a telling gap. Every single one required an account. None showed money spent clearly. None had category breakdowns. All had reminders, but the financial transparency and cancellation support that users actually needed was missing across the board.

Chapter 03

Defining
the user.

— Two people, one shared anxiety.

— personas
Worried about where it's going.
PERSONA 01

Dia, 25, software engineer

Based in Bangalore, loves streaming and music. Her card is directly linked to her subscriptions and she only finds out about renewals when the money is deducted. She wants to spend mindfully but has no visibility into whether her subscriptions are worth what she's paying.

PERSONA 02

Nayan, 32, professor

Based in Mumbai with two teenage kids, with subscriptions for both personal use and teaching. His university reimburses some subscriptions, making tracking even more complex. He wants oversight without having to dig through bank statements every month.

The empathy map built from these users told the same story: people are worried about where their money is going, questioning whether their subscriptions are worth it, and doing workarounds like keeping manual financial lists and checking card transactions because no tool was doing this for them.

Chapter 04

What I designed.

— Home, Analytics, Subscriptions, Alerts.

— design
Four areas, one clear picture.

The information architecture centred on four core areas: Home, Analytics, Subscriptions, and Profile.

  • Home
    A dashboard showing money spent this month, upcoming bills, and smart recommendations. The first screen you open should tell you immediately what's happening with your subscriptions and what's coming next.
  • Analytics
    A visual breakdown of spending through graphs and category-based pie charts. Weekly, monthly, and annual views so you can see patterns, not just numbers. This was the feature missing from every competitor I analysed.
  • Subscriptions
    A complete list of all your subscriptions with full details: category, plan type, price, billing cycle, auto-renewal status, and transaction history. Add, pause, change, or delete a subscription all in one place, each linking directly to the service's cancellation page.
  • Alerts
    Proactive notifications before a payment is due, not after. You choose when you want to be reminded.

The user flow went from onboarding through to the full subscription management experience, with bills due, offers, and news surfaced contextually so the app felt useful beyond just tracking.

Process

I sketched wireframes across all key screens before moving to UI: onboarding, login, home, analytics, subscription list, subscription detail card, add subscription, and profile. The sketching phase was where most of the structural decisions got made: what lives on home vs. analytics, how to surface upcoming bills without overwhelming the dashboard, how to make adding a subscription feel fast.

The final UI used a dark theme with high contrast, deliberate for a fintech context where clarity and trust matter more than decoration.

— reflection
A shared, universal anxiety.

Two weeks is enough time to go deep if you stay focused. The competitive analysis was the most valuable single exercise in this project; seeing that none of the existing apps showed money spent clearly made the design direction obvious. Sometimes the gap in the market is right there in a comparison table.

I also learnt that subscription anxiety is more universal than I expected. Everyone I spoke to had a version of the same problem. That's usually a good sign you're working on something worth building.
