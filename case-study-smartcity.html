Chapter 01

The problem.

— Smarter for whom?

— the problem
A default citizen who isn't everyone.

Every year, more people leave small towns for big cities chasing jobs, comfort, and opportunity. The cities they leave behind struggle with declining populations and crumbling infrastructure. Smart city technology is supposed to help, but most smart city design makes the same mistake as urban planning always has: it's built around a default citizen that doesn't represent everyone.

We wanted to ask a different question. Not "how do we make cities smarter?" but "smarter for whom?"

Chapter 02

The approach.

— Speculative design, and six feminist HCI values.

— approach & method
Imagining before critiquing.

We used speculative design, a method that invites people to imagine futures rather than just critique the present. It's deliberately open-ended, sometimes even absurd, because the point isn't to get practical answers immediately. It's to surface values, fears, and desires that more conventional research methods miss.

We ran three workshops in Weimar with nine local residents, embedding feminist HCI principles into every step. These weren't just theoretical labels: they shaped how we recruited participants, how we structured activities, and what questions we asked when things got uncomfortable.

What we did

Before the workshop, participants chose two photographs — one representing their vision of a utopian city, one dystopian — and took a walk around Weimar, noting what they wished was different.

01

Vision boards

Built from their utopian and dystopian photographs.

02

Speculative stories

Written, or drawn as comic strips, set in future cities.

03

Town walkthrough

Grounded the imagination in the real place participants live.

04

Group discussion

Observations shared and compared as a group.

The prompt we gave for the storytelling session started in a stone hut with no running water, deliberately primitive, to make participants work their way forward toward what they actually value in a city.

Chapter 03

What emerged.

— Utopias that felt human, not optimised.

— what emerged
Not what smart city marketing promises.

What people wanted from a utopian small city was not what smart city marketing usually promises. Participants talked about nature integrated into everyday life, genuinely safe public spaces, direct democracy, freedom of expression, and spaces that support mental wellbeing. They wanted cities that felt human, not optimised.

What people feared in a dystopian city was more revealing: excessive surveillance, technology used for control, loss of human connection, censorship, social scoring. The dystopia they imagined wasn't a broken city; it was a city that had prioritised efficiency over people.

A bio-degradable vehicle made of spaghetti, running on tomato juice. Mobile swamps to provide greenery and prevent flooding.

The speculative stories produced were inventive, including a political scenario where opposing ideologies reached peaceful compromise. Absurd on the surface, but each one was pointing at something real: sustainability, ecological integration, democratic values.

Chapter 04

What the method
revealed.

— Honest limitations, published alongside the findings.

— reflection on method
The limits, published too.

Speculative design worked well at surfacing values and sparking imagination; participants engaged deeply, and the outputs were genuinely diverse. But it also had honest limitations. The outcomes were sometimes too abstract to act on directly. Participants' personal biases shaped what they imagined as utopia. And recruiting residents of a small town who had mostly grown up in larger cities meant the town's specific problems were sometimes hard for them to identify.

These tensions are part of what we published, not just the findings, but a critical reflection on the method itself.

What I learnt

Speculative design is one of the few methods that lets participants dream before they self-censor. But running it well requires real facilitation skill: knowing when to push someone's idea further, when to ask "for whom would this be a dystopia?", and when to let the absurdity play out because it's pointing somewhere important.

This project also deepened my belief that the most interesting design problems aren't technical; they're political. Who gets to imagine the future, and whose imagination gets built?
